# Path A — Building Hypotheses from Scratch

Load this when the founder does not have existing hypotheses. Work through all three categories
in order: Customer first, then Problem, then Solution. **Do not announce category names out
loud.** Ask one question per message. Extract and internally log each hypothesis as it surfaces.
Stop when you have 6–8 solid hypotheses across all three categories — do not push for more.

---

## Category 1 — Customer Hypotheses

**Goal:** Surface assumptions about who the customer actually is — their role, behavior,
context, and decision-making authority. This category comes first because the problem only
becomes real once you know whose problem it is.

**Questions to draw from (ask one at a time, in order):**

- "Who specifically has this problem — not a segment, one person. Describe them: their role,
  their company size, their day when the problem bites hardest."
- "Is the person who feels the pain the same person who would buy your product?
  Or is there a buyer and a user who are different people?"
- "What does this person's organization look like — are they a solo operator, part of a small
  team, inside a large enterprise? Does that change the problem?"
- "Are there meaningfully different types of customers who experience this problem differently —
  say, a small business versus a large one, or an early-career person versus a senior one?
  How different is their situation?"
- "What would make this person willing to pay — and what would make them walk away even if
  the product worked?"

**What to extract.** Log each assumption in this format:
`We believe our primary customer is [X], characterized by [Y]. We have [strong / moderate / weak / no] evidence for this.`

**Important:** A founder who assumes small businesses and enterprises have the same problem
is holding a hypothesis that needs testing. Flag this explicitly when it comes up.

**If discovery data has a clear customer signal:** anchor it — "Your data mentions [customer
type from evidence] — is that the person, or is the problem sharpest for someone else?"

Move to Category 2 when you have 2–3 customer hypotheses and the founder has described a
specific person or situation (not a category).

---

## Category 2 — Problem Hypotheses

**Goal:** Surface assumptions about whether the problem is real, how painful it is, how
frequently it occurs, and whether people are actively trying to solve it. Now that you have
the customer anchored, probe the problem through that specific person's lens.

**Questions to draw from (ask one at a time, in order):**

- "Describe the problem your [Category 1 customer] faces — not what your product does,
  but the situation they're in when things go wrong. What exactly breaks down?"
- "How often does this problem hit them? Daily, weekly, once a quarter?"
- "How do you know this is painful enough that someone would pay to fix it —
  have you seen evidence of that, or is that an assumption?"
- "What does this person do when the problem shows up today? Walk me through
  their workaround, even if it's ugly."
- "Is this a problem people know they have — or one they've normalized and stopped noticing?"

**What to extract.** Log each assumption in this format:
`We believe [X] is true about the problem. We have [strong / moderate / weak / no] evidence for this.`

**If discovery data has a clear problem signal:** anchor it — "Your data shows [strongest pain
signal from evidence] as the recurring friction — is that the core problem, or is something
else sharper?"

**Competitor in the workaround.** If a named competitor appears (e.g. "they use Salesforce",
"they're on Notion"), do not move past the workaround. Probe the differentiation:
> "They're already using [competitor] for this — what does [competitor] not do for them?
> What's the gap that makes them still have the problem?"
If the founder can't answer, log it as a Critical hypothesis:
`We believe [competitor] does not fully solve this problem for our customer. We have no evidence for this.`
Flag it explicitly: "This needs to be your first interview question. If [competitor] already
solves it, that changes everything."

Move to Category 3 when you have 2–3 problem hypotheses with specific frequency and workaround.

---

## Category 3 — Solution Hypotheses

**Goal:** Surface assumptions about whether the founder's proposed solution actually solves
the job, whether customers will change behavior to adopt it, and whether the business model
works. This category comes last because it's only meaningful once Customer and Problem are clear.

**Questions to draw from (ask one at a time, in order):**

- "What does your solution actually do for the customer — what is their life or work like
  after they use it, compared to before?"
- "What behavior change does your solution require from the customer?
  What are they giving up or doing differently?"
- "Why would a customer switch to your solution from whatever they do today?
  What's the switching cost — and is your solution worth it?"
- "What does your pricing model assume about the customer's willingness to pay —
  and have you tested that assumption with a real number in a real conversation?"
- "Is there a part of your solution that depends on a technology, partnership,
  or capability you don't fully control yet?"

**What to extract.** Log each assumption in this format:
`We believe customers will [behavior X] in response to our solution. We have [strong / moderate / weak / no] evidence for this.`

**Flag if Solution hypotheses dominate.** If the founder's list is mostly Solution with thin
Customer and Problem coverage, say:
> "I notice most of these are about your product, not your customer's world. Let's back up —
> what do you believe is true about the customer and the problem before your solution enters
> the picture?"

Move to Ranking (in the main SKILL, load `reference/ranking-and-questions.md`) when you have
6–8 hypotheses total across all three categories and the founder has confirmed the list.
