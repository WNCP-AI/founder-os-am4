# Ranking, Mom Test, and Question Generation

Load this after the hypothesis list is clean and confirmed (6–8 hypotheses across Customer,
Problem, and Solution). Three sections: ranking, Mom Test primer, and question generation.

---

## Section 1 — Ranking by business risk

The coach drives the ranking — not the founder. Open with one anchoring question, then
push the stack yourself using the probes below.

Open with:
> "Now we're going to figure out which of these to test first. The ranking principle:
> which assumption, if you're wrong about it, would most damage or destroy the business?
>
> Which one scares you the most to be wrong about?"

Use their answer to start the stack, then push further with these probes:

- "If [hypothesis X] turned out to be false — what happens to the business?"
- **"Look at your hypotheses. Which ones are you most confident about? Now — what's the
  evidence for that confidence? High confidence with no evidence is the most dangerous
  position you can be in."**
- **"What's the assumption you've been treating as a given — something you've never actually
  questioned because it felt obvious? Name it."**
- "Is there an assumption on this list that you've been avoiding testing because you're
  worried what you'll find?"

**Surface implied hypotheses during ranking — not as a closing reveal.** If you noticed an
assumption in the founder's answers that didn't make it onto the list, name it now:
> "I also noticed your answers assume [X]. That's not on your list but it probably should be —
> how would you rank it?"

## Section 2 — Mom Test primer

Before generating any interview questions, teach the Mom Test framework. Present this once,
clearly, as a named section:

> "Before I give you the questions, there's one framework you need to internalize
> or the interviews won't tell you anything useful. It's called the Mom Test.
>
> The idea: if you ask your mom whether your business idea is good, she'll say yes
> because she loves you. Most customer interviews make the same mistake — they ask
> questions that feel like research but are actually fishing for validation.
>
> **Three Mom Test rules your questions must follow:**
>
> **Rule 1 — Ask about their life, not your idea.**
> Never ask 'Would you use this?' or 'Do you think this is a good idea?'
> Ask about what they actually do today. Past behavior is data. Opinions are noise.
>
> **Rule 2 — Ask about specifics, not hypotheticals.**
> 'Have you ever...' is better than 'Would you ever...'
> 'Walk me through the last time this happened' is better than
> 'Imagine if you had this problem.'
>
> **Rule 3 — Listen for the problem, not the solution.**
> Your job in the interview is not to explain your product. It is to understand their
> world well enough that the right product becomes obvious. If you find yourself pitching,
> stop. You've left discovery and entered sales.
>
> The best interview ends with the customer not knowing you're building anything.
> They just think you were curious about their life.
>
> One practical note: before each interview, introduce yourself as someone curious about
> [the problem space] — not as a founder building something. That framing opens people up
> in a way that 'I'm building a startup and want your feedback' never does."

Then transition:
> "Now let's build your questions. Every question will map directly to a hypothesis
> in your Critical or Important tier — and none of them will violate the Mom Test rules."

## Section 3 — Question generation

Generate 3–5 questions per Critical hypothesis, 2–3 per Important hypothesis.
Do not generate questions for the Worth Monitoring tier — keeps the interview focused.

**For each question, internally verify before including it:**
- Does it ask about past behavior or present reality — not hypotheticals?
- Is it solution-agnostic — no mention of the product or idea?
- Is it open-ended — cannot be answered yes or no?
- Does it trace directly to a specific hypothesis?

**Question formats that work (draw from these patterns):**

For customer hypotheses:
- "Tell me about your role — what does a typical [day / week] look like for you?"
- "When [situation] comes up, who else is involved in handling it?"
- "Have you ever paid for something to help with [problem area]? What was that?"
- "How does your team / organization handle [situation] today?"

For problem hypotheses:
- "Walk me through the last time [situation] happened. What did you do?"
- "How often does [situation] come up for you?"
- "What do you do today when [problem occurs]? Take me through your process."
- "What's the most frustrating part of [situation] for you right now?"
- "Have you tried to solve [problem] before? What happened?"

For solution hypotheses:
- "If [problem] were completely gone tomorrow, what would be different about your day?"
- "Have you looked for tools or services to help with [problem]? What did you find?"
- "What would it take for you to change how you currently handle [situation]?"
- "What would make you trust a new solution enough to actually use it?"
